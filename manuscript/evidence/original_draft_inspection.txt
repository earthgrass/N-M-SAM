APBR-SAM: Adaptive Perturbation Budget Reallocation for
Post-Training N:M Structured Pruning
Abstract
Sharpness-aware minimization (SAM) explores local parameter perturbations during dense training, while post-
training N:M pruning deletes weights through finite, discrete changes to their support. Scale alone does not capture the
mismatch. Conventional SAM distributes its fixed perturbation budget across all coordinates, including weights that
will be retained, whereas an N:M deletion direction is supported only on the removed coordinates. Perturbing retained
coordinates can still benefit dense optimization, but it is directionally redundant for representing that structured
deletion. We study how this finite budget can instead be spent to learn a geometry that informs the subsequent
support decision.
We propose Adaptive Perturbation Budget Reallocation for SAM (APBR-SAM). Rather than enlarging the per-
turbation space, APBR-SAM reallocates the same finite global L2 budget across coordinates. It maintains persistent
positive scales that reweight SAM’s first gradient while keeping the realized radius fixed. Feedback decreases the
relative scales of coordinates that repeatedly receive above-average perturbation energy and increases those receiving
below-average energy, so the scales settle into an anisotropic geometry over the training trajectory. After training,
weight magnitude divided by the learned scale ranks coordinates within each N:M group, transferring the geometric
state into a structured mask.
We formalize the transfer with a normalized deletion cost. At fixed weights and scales, the ranking minimizes that
cost under the group constraints; with a shared curvature bound along candidate deletion segments, it also minimizes
a common upper bound on pruning-induced loss increase. Experiments compare immediate pruning performance and
fixed-mask recovery against magnitude pruning, activation-aware scoring, and second-order compensation baselines.
APBR-SAM thus connects the allocation of training perturbations to the structured parameter changes imposed after
training.
I. Introduction
Post-training N:M pruning converts a dense model into a structured sparse model by retaining N coordinates in each
group of M weights. The local pattern supports structured sparse execution [1], but imposes a discrete decision about
which learned weights to remove. Each candidate mask changes the parameter support and produces a specific deletion
displacement. The quality of the final model therefore depends on both the dense training trajectory and the support
selected after it.
SAM offers a natural starting point for robust dense training [2]. It uses the current mini-batch gradient to ap-
proximate a worst-case direction in a local Euclidean ball, then updates the model using a second gradient at the
perturbed weights. Post-training pruning produces a different kind of parameter change: selected coordinates move
all the way to zero, with their locations constrained by the N:M pattern. The displacement is finite, determined by
the removed weights, and unrestricted by SAM’s training radius. Its direction is set by a discrete mask instead of
the current adversarial-gradient construction. Moreover, this deletion direction has nonzero support only on removed
weights, whereas the dense SAM perturbation generally spends part of its fixed norm budget on coordinates that the
eventual mask retains. Retained-coordinate perturbations still help dense generalization; they are only directionally
redundant for representing the structured deletion itself. Local perturbation optimization therefore leaves an explicit
scale, direction, and support mismatch between the changes explored during training and those later applied to the
model.
A second gap concerns how this limited budget is allocated and what information survives into pruning. Conven-
tional SAM uses a single Euclidean constraint and normalizes the current stochastic gradient, so its finite energy is
consumed by the instantaneous direction across both future-removed and future-retained coordinates. The rule main-
tains no persistent coordinate-scale state describing how that allocation evolves across mini-batches. A subsequent
magnitude mask therefore ranks the final weights independently of the exploration history. A shared radius answers
how far the local probe can move. It does not align the probe with a support-constrained deletion, nor does it specify
how accumulated coordinate geometry should influence which positions are retained within a group.
These gaps motivate a different design question: can the same finite perturbation budget be redistributed across
coordinates during training so that its allocation becomes useful for N:M support selection? Changing a common
1
radius only changes the overall exploration scale. Learning coordinate scales instead changes how the fixed budget
is spent and turns that allocation into a persistent state that pruning can use. Rather than predicting a mask at every
training step, the method learns a geometry that connects local exploration with the later deletion decision, and it keeps
SAM’s two-gradient update throughout.
APBR-SAM implements this idea with positive coordinate scales initialized to the identity geometry. The scales
reweight the first SAM gradient, and global L2 normalization fixes the actual perturbation radius. Each step measures
how much of the perturbation energy each coordinate carries. Negative feedback decreases scales with above-average
energy share and increases those with below-average share, altering their future directional weights. The state is
updated recursively throughout training, so it records the feedback trajectory rather than being assigned from a single
final gradient.
After dense training, APBR-SAM retains this state with the model weights and uses their ratio to construct the
N:M mask. Deleting a weight moves its coordinate by its full magnitude; dividing that magnitude by the learned scale
expresses the deletion in geometry-normalized coordinates. Selecting the largest ratios within each group exactly
minimizes the corresponding normalized squared deletion cost. Thus the final score is the support-selection stage of
the same geometry-learning process, rather than a detached post-training rule.
The theory makes this connection precise at two levels. We characterize the feedback dynamics under a fixed
nonzero gradient, and we derive the deletion-cost interpretation of the ranking.
Under a common transformed-
curvature bound along all candidate deletion segments, that cost controls an upper bound on the loss increase. The
same groupwise ranking minimizes this common bound with the weights and geometry fixed. Actual post-pruning
accuracy and recovery remain the empirical questions.
Our completed CIFAR-100 comparisons evaluate them
against magnitude and activation-aware pruning and second-order compensation baselines under a fixed-final-artifact
protocol.
Our contributions are:
• A persistent coordinate-geometry mechanism that reallocates a finite actual L2 perturbation budget across co-
ordinates using global energy feedback, rather than enlarging the perturbation radius, and preserves the product
of coordinate scales in exact arithmetic.
• A training-to-pruning transfer through normalized weight scores, with groupwise deletion-cost optimality and
a conditional common loss-increment upper bound.
• A structured-pruning evaluation that follows dense models through immediate pruning and fixed-mask recovery,
reporting complete-method comparisons.
II. Related Work
Sharpness-aware optimization.
SAM uses a first gradient to construct a local worst-case perturbation and a second
gradient to update the model [2]. ASAM makes the neighborhood invariant to parameter rescaling [3], while SSAM
sparsifies the perturbation through Fisher-guided or dynamic masks [4]. GEAR-SAM reallocates a fixed perturbation
budget across parameter blocks using an exponential moving average of gradient energy [5]. APBR-SAM instead
learns persistent coordinate-level scales and transfers the resulting geometry from dense training to post-training sup-
port selection.
Compression-aware training.
CrAM incorporates a target compression operator into optimization and studies one-
shot N:M pruning [6]. AdaSAP couples adaptive perturbations with channel pruning and fine-tuning [7]. These
methods expose the model to compression or pruning during optimization. APBR-SAM instead keeps dense two-pass
optimization and constructs the discrete N:M mask only after dense training.
Post-training weight selection and compensation.
Magnitude pruning removes small connections and commonly
recovers the retained weights by fine-tuning [8]. Wanda augments weight magnitude with input-activation norms
but performs no pruning-time weight reconstruction [9]. SparseGPT uses calibration activations and an approxi-
mate inverse-Hessian procedure to select weights and compensate pruning error in one shot [10]; ROSE reorders
SparseGPT’s pruning sequence according to estimated column and block losses [11]. OBC develops the related Exac-
tOBS framework for post-training pruning and quantization [12]. For N:M sparsity, SR-STE instead trains the sparse
model from scratch [1]. APBR-SAM differs in that its ranking state is learned during dense SAM training and then
frozen as the support used in recovery.

F. From dense training to sparse recovery
The pipeline retains the information needed to transfer between stages. Dense training saves unperturbed weights
together with their matching geometric state. The selected checkpoint supplies both w and s for Eq. (8). The learned
state is therefore used in the support decision after local exploration ends. A one-time mask application produces
the Raw Hard model, retaining the parent’s BN buffers. Fixed-mask recovery initializes a fresh SGD optimizer and
updates sparse weights under the fixed support, with geometry held fixed. Immediate pruning performance tests the
selected support before weight adaptation; recovery measures the quality reachable under a shared optimization budget.

IV. Experiments
Setup.
We evaluate ResNet-18 on CIFAR-100 and compare a SAM baseline, Wanda [9], SparseGPT [10],
ROSE [11], and APBR-SAM under the same structured sparsity target. The SAM baseline prunes the final Dense-
SAM checkpoint by magnitude [8], whereas APBR-SAM applies the learned score to its own final checkpoint. Every
sparse model is fine-tuned for 20 epochs with its mask fixed. We conduct three independent runs and report mean
accuracy and sample standard deviation; complete implementation details are provided in Appendix E.
